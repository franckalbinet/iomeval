Coordination with GIZ was complex due to its dual role as both a grant provider and an implementing partner. This dual function led to role ambiguity, as GIZ oversaw other implementing partners while also running its own activities, sometimes risking having overlapping responsibilities and parallel structures with IOM. Also, some interviewees mentioned that the lack of country-level GIZ coordinators in South Sudan complicated collaboration. Despite these challenges, importantly, both GIZ and IOM maintained a close and professional relationship, described by interviewees on both sides as “cordial”, “appreciated”, “very cooperative”, and “smooth” – something which ultimately helped navigating the relationship and avoid inefficiencies.

Finally, IOM has established an efficient and structured monitoring and reporting system under the BMM programme, ensuring regular tracking of progress and challenges to adhere to the heavy reporting requirements. Reporting requirements were described by IOM staff and implementing partners as “very straightforward”, with interim reports proving useful in documenting key achievements and identifying implementation challenges. The log frame was strengthened by IOM following the start of the project to connect programme outputs and activities to Annual Work Plans. Noteworthy, a stronger integration of qualitative assessments, including beneficiary perspectives, could enhance the depth of analysis. Although quantitative indicators provide valuable insights, they may not fully capture the programme's broader, long-term impact. Additionally, while the IOM digital platform ‘Sharepoint’ is beneficial for document management, ensuring that all staff utilise it remains a challenge. Nonetheless, IOM’s emphasis on systematic data collection and accessible reporting structures has strengthened programme oversight and adaptive management.

The following section assesses to what extent the BMM programme (Phase III) implemented by IOM has made progress towards its overall objective, and whether further impact can be expected in the upcoming years. To be sure, evaluating impact, which focuses on the broader, long-term effects of an intervention, presents methodological challenges, as the evaluation design did not include field visits and direct engagement with further beneficiaries (migrants). The intended impact of the BMM Programme Phase III according to its Theory of Change was to create a sustainable and well-managed (“safe, orderly and regular”) migration environment in the EHoA region. This was to be achieved through the three key components: enhanced migration governance, effective institutions and improved cross-border cooperation, and protection of vulnerable migrants.

IOM has made substantial positive contributions to strengthening migration governance, border management, and migrant protection across the region. By enhancing governance frameworks, fostering cross-border cooperation, improving protection mechanisms, raising awareness, and building institutional capacity, IOM activities have contributed to safer, more orderly, and regular migration.

The collected evaluation data confirms significant contributions that were made by IOM to improving migration governance, border management, and migrant protection in the region. While the long-term impact remains challenging to fully assess, the available evidence highlights meaningful progress towards the programme’s objectives.
