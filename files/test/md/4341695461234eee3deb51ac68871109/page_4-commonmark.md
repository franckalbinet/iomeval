

The East and Horn of Africa is a region that serves as a point of
origin, transit, and destination for migration. Here, migration arises
from the adverse effects of climate change, conflict and instability,
exacerbating socioeconomic challenges. Migrants in the region are highly
vulnerable to exploitation, trafficking and other dangers related to
migration in the region.¹

In 2016, the International Organization for Migration (IOM), alongside
the British Council, CIVIPOL, GIZ and United Nations Office on Drugs and
Crimes (UNODC), launched the Better Migration Management (BMM) programme
in Djibouti, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Kenya, Somalia, South Sudan, Sudan and
Uganda. The programme was funded by the European Union (EU) Trust Fund
for Africa and the German Federal Ministry for Economic Cooperation and
Development. Since 2016, BMM has witnessed three phases.

The overall objective of BMM is to enable national authorities and
institutions to manage safe, orderly and regular migration by applying a
human rights-based approach and addressing human trafficking and
smuggling of migrants. The programme comprises three interconnected
components:

1.  Strengthening migration governance to create legal frameworks for
    regionally coordinated migration management;
2.  Supporting institutions to combat human trafficking and smuggling of
    migrants through integrated border governance; and
3.  Protecting victims of trafficking and vulnerable migrants.

GIZ serves as the lead implementer of the programme, contracting IOM to
implement activities under each of these three components. The third
phase of the BMM programme commenced in October 2022 and is scheduled to
conclude in March 2025, with the majority of activities completed by
December 2024.

IOM commissioned an independent final evaluation to assess its
contributions under Phase III. This evaluation, conducted by two
external consultants, examines the relevance, coherence, effectiveness,
efficiency, impact, and sustainability of IOM’s interventions. It
applies a theory-based approach, using the Theory of Change as a
foundation and aligning with OECD DAC evaluation criteria and UN
Evaluation Group (UNEG) norms and standards

The evaluation employed a mixed-methods approach, including desk
research and key informant interviews. Desk research was conducted in
two phases to ensure data triangulation, while 40 online interviews were
carried out with IOM staff, implementing partners, national government
stakeholders, and civil society organizations.
