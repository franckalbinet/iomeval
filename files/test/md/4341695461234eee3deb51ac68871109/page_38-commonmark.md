As one interviewed staff member summarized it: “I would say the support
was fantastic. In terms of budget support, in terms of technical

In terms of external coordination, BMM Phase III required cooperation
with other implementing partners, donors, and national governments. All
implementing partners (at the regional level) were meeting twice a year
through a Steering Committee in Brussels and at the national level in
each country monthly (apart from South Sudan which is missing that
structure); these meetings were chaired and led by GIZ. Similarly to the
internal coordination, some interviewees also mentioned the need for
country office staff from different partner organizations to meet with
each other.

One interviewee noted: “I do not know if \[implementing partners\] have
ever all sat together, you know, to discuss BMM, and I mean all
countries together. This could be done online… It does not have to be a
super big event, just an exchange.”

In this regard, IOM worked alongside all BMM implementing partners,
although to a different degree. IOM and UNODC maintained a clear
division of labor, with UNODC focusing on legal frameworks and criminal
justice, while IOM concentrated on victim protection and migration
governance. One interviewee explained: “UNODC developed a curriculum on
trafficking in persons for the National School of the Judiciary, while
\[IOM\] trained stakeholders on victim identification and referral.”
Collaboration with CIVIPOL seemed to have improved over time,
particularly in areas such as multi-agency simulations and training
programs. Coordination with the British Council was less extensive, as
their focus on community engagement differed from IOM’s policy-level
work. One interviewee highlighted this distinction: “\[British Council\]
activities are distinct and complement each other. IOM works more on the
higher policy level, while British Council focuses more on community
engagement.”

While joint activities were conducted, some interviewees noted that the
monthly coordination meetings chaired by GIZ in all countries except
South Sudan were primarily used for information sharing rather than
strategic planning. One stakeholder suggested: “the monthly coordination
meetings \[chaired by GIZ\]… could be a forum where there is also some
discussion on the contents and some initiatives for joint planning.” For
instance, an interviewee suggested that partners could better coordinate
their activities in terms of implementation locations: “If we agree on
the selection of the region, that would amplify the result, the impact
because IOM works with the government, and we work with the community.”
Some interviewees also noted that coordination between partners was
sometimes more efficient when organizations met separately to address
specific issues or common activities bilaterally.

Another challenge in external coordination was the differing project
timelines among implementing partners. Some organizations had different
start and end dates for their Phase III activities, leading to
challenges in planning and aligning for joint initiatives.
